Lesson 1 · Product Thinking

Problems, not solutions

Learn to spot a solution dressed up as a problem.

≈ 10 minutes Based on IDG Module 1 Skill: reframe a request as a problem

The idea

The Institute of Digital Government (IDG) defines product thinking as “a discipline of asking better questions before reaching for solutions.” [IDG M7] The first and most important question is what problem are we actually solving?

That sounds obvious. In practice, teams often skip it. IDG points out that a project can fail even when it is run well, if it is “solving the wrong problem.” [IDG M1]

When we skip proper problem definition, we risk building the wrong thing entirely: on time, on budget, and completely unhelpful. IDG, Module 1: Understanding the Problem

The Common Trap

A lot of requests sound like problems but are really solutions. IDG calls these “solutions in disguise” and gives these examples [IDG M1]:

What we hearThe real issue
“We need more AI.”Officers struggle to make decisions from large, unwieldy datasets.
“We need a superapp.”Citizens can’t access services efficiently across platforms.
“We don’t have a CRM.”Cumbersome processes cause long wait times for citizens.

Look at the difference. The left column names a thing to build. The right column names a person, what they are trying to do, and what gets in their way. IDG’s first principle asks exactly this:

The three questions

“Who are our users, what are they trying to do, and what’s getting in their way?” [IDG M1]

A quick test: if the statement could only be fixed one way, it’s probably a solution. “We need a superapp” can only be fixed by a superapp. “Citizens can’t access services efficiently” could be fixed in many ways, and some of them might be cheaper or better than an app.

Why this matters for your career

PM interviews often start with prompts like “design an app for X” or “how would you improve Y?”. The prompt itself is a solution in disguise. Before you suggest any features, use the three questions: who is the user, what are they trying to do, and what’s in their way. That is product thinking out loud.

This is IDG’s first principle. The other two are chase outcomes, not outputs and integrate policy, ops and tech. They get their own lessons later.

Practice 1 · Problem or solution?

Here are six statements you might hear at work. Decide whether each one is a real Problem or a Solution in disguise.

1. “Our division needs a dashboard.”

2. “Seniors give up renewing their concession card online because the login step fails halfway.”

3. “Let’s add a chatbot to the feedback page.”

4. “Parents miss school consent deadlines because the paper slips get lost in their children’s bags.”

5. “Our booking system is outdated, so we need to migrate to the cloud.”

6. “Small-business owners spend hours working out which government grants they qualify for.”

Practice 2 · Reframe it

Your manager says: “We need a mobile app for our community centre.” Rewrite it as a problem using the three questions. You don’t know the real facts, so make up plausible ones. The goal is practising the shape.

One possible reframe

“Working parents (who) want to sign their children up for holiday programmes (trying to do), but registration only happens in person during office hours, so popular classes fill up before they can get there (what’s in the way).”

Check your version:

  • Does it name a specific user? “Residents” is weaker than “working parents”.
  • Does it say what they are trying to do?
  • Does it say what is getting in their way?
  • Is it free of solutions? If the word “app” is still in it, try again.

Notice that once the problem is clear, an app might not be the best fix. Online registration on the existing website could solve it more cheaply.

Before you close this page

Look away from the screen and answer this from memory: what are the three questions that turn a solution into a problem? Say them out loud. Next time, we’ll start with this question before anything new.
